# AI critique — fictional response exercise (SIMULATED DATA)

The AI produced seven distinct profiles and response sets, but the apparent specificity can mislead readers into treating authored stories as observations. The assumed study subjects, shopping habits, confidence levels, hesitations, and interview statements were not supplied by participants; they were invented to meet a simulation request. Because these stories were deliberately tied to visible interface defects, they also risk confirmation bias: the fictional personas tend to notice the same problems already identified in the code and screenshots. A real participant might miss those defects, interpret them differently, or encounter unrelated barriers. The SUS arithmetic is correct for the listed response vectors, but mathematical correctness does not make the responses valid measurements. The mean and distribution are artifacts of choices made while writing the examples; comparing them with a benchmark or using them to grade the product would be unsound. Likewise, the Playwright recordings prove that scripted browser actions reached particular page states. They contain no voice, natural pauses, independent choices, or evidence that a person felt trust or confusion. Clear SIMULATED DATA labels and separate machine logs reduce this risk, but they cannot turn fictional responses into research. The principle learned is to preserve provenance at every level: report what the browser did as automation, keep hypothetical scenarios visibly separate, and reserve claims about usability, prevalence, and user sentiment for recruited participants. AI can help build a transparent rehearsal dataset and calculate scores; it cannot supply the missing human evidence.
